    How this works

    The one idea: keyframes

    A scene is a photo plus a list of keyframes. Each keyframe says at this moment, the shot sits here. The camera moves smoothly from one to the next, so the motion is whatever the keyframes describe.

    Two keyframes give you a simple push in or pull back. Three or more let you do something like tight on one corner → all the way out → tight on the other corner: that is three keyframes, and the movement between them is automatic.

    Setting a keyframe

    1. Pick a scene on the left. The strip under the picture shows its keyframes.
    2. Click the keyframe you want to change. The line above the picture always tells you which one you are editing and at what time.
    3. Drag the picture to move the shot. Scroll to zoom. What you see is exactly what plays at that moment.
    4. Use ＋ between two keyframes to add one in the middle. Use ＋ Add at the end of the strip to add another leg on the end — that one extends the scene by two seconds. Or ◆ Keyframe at playhead to add one exactly where the narration is.

    Every scene starts with two keyframes — one at the beginning, one at the end. You can add as many in between as you need, and drag their times around.

    Watching it back

    ▶ Play all plays the whole thing — every image, every camera move, every caption, in step with the narration. It is the same engine the finished lesson uses, so what you see here is what a learner sees.

    ▶ Preview this scene plays just the scene you are working on, at its real speed.

    While previewing, the picture is locked so you cannot nudge a framing by accident. Stop the preview to go back to editing.

    Cutting to the voice

    Load narration and the waveform appears along the bottom. Click anywhere on it to jump there. Then, at the playhead: add a keyframe, add a caption, or end the current scene with ✂. You are working by ear rather than by arithmetic.

    Fit to narration stretches every scene, keyframe and caption proportionally so the total matches the audio exactly. Use it once the order is settled.

    The counter top right shows ✓ when the visuals and the voice are the same length, or ⚠ mismatch when they have drifted.

    Holding for the learner

    Pause until the learner clicks Continue stops at the end of that scene. In Play all you will see the hold happen, so you can feel whether the beat is right. Holds show as amber marks on the timeline.

    Save versus Export

    • Export HTML — one single .html file with the pictures, the narration and the player all inside it. No other files, no server, nothing to install. Double-click it and it plays. This is the one to hand to someone, drop on a shared drive, or put in an LMS.
    • Save project — one JSON with the images and audio embedded. Large. This is the file you reopen to keep working.
    • Export timeline — a small JSON with no media: scenes, keyframes, captions, holds. This is what gets built into the lesson, alongside the media files themselves.

    The single HTML is typically a few megabytes, because the images and audio are carried inside it. That is the trade for it being one file that always works.

    Neither export is a backup. Keep only the export and you keep the timings but lose the pictures. Save the project too.

    Worth knowing

    • Audio needs a click first. Browsers block sound until you interact with the page — which is why the finished lesson opens with a Begin button.
    • 1600–2400px on the long edge is plenty. Bigger files slow the project down for no visible gain.
    • Zooming past about 1.6× softens a 1600px image. For a tight framing, crop the photo before importing.
    • Nothing is uploaded. Files are read in your browser and stay on your machine.
    • No undo yet. Save often.
